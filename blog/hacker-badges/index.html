2,500 Hacker Badges

Taking a hardware badge from schematic to volume production, and everything that went wrong on the way

Written a week before the event. Every badge is built, shipped, and sitting in a shipping container in Waterloo. Two thousand people are about to turn them on for the first time. I’ll update this once I know how that went.

What I built

A hardware badge for Hack the North. 2,500 of them.

It’s a PCB the size of a paperback — 148 × 90 mm — with a colour LCD, a D-pad, four buttons, six RGB LEDs, an NFC reader, Bluetooth, and two AA batteries on the back. It runs an ESP32-C3. It boots into an app launcher. Hackers can write their own apps for it in Lua, from a browser IDE, at the event.

No enclosure. Bare board on a lanyard, raw like an old-school DEFCON badge. The board is the product.

I’m a software engineer. I had never manufactured anything. Here’s what that cost me.

Four boards, and what each one was for

The single most useful thing I did all year was refuse to build the real thing first.

BoardOrderedArrivedQtyThe question it answered
EVTFeb 15Mar 3a handfulDo these parts work at all?
DVTApr 3Apr 23a handfulDoes the real design work?
PVTMay 14Jun 250Can they build it?
ProductionJun 30Aug 202,465—

Each one exists to kill a different kind of unknown. EVT is “is my schematic wrong.” DVT is “is my layout wrong.” PVT is “is my factory wrong.” They are not the same question and you cannot answer them at the same time.

I nearly skipped the PVT to save three weeks. If I had, I’d have found out about the flipped chips on 1,700 boards instead of 50.

The EVT was a hexagon with every functional block labelled on the silkscreen — Power, NFC, ESP32, Flash, Programming Mode. That labelling was not decoration. When you’re bringing up a board at 2am, being able to point at a region and know what it is saves real time.

The mistakes that were mine

10u is not 10uH

The EVT wouldn’t run off batteries. USB was fine.

The boost converter that takes 2×AA up to 5V needs an inductor. I put one in the schematic and labelled the value 10u. I meant 10 microhenries, because it’s an inductor. JLC’s automatic part matcher read 10u and gave me a 10 microfarad capacitor.

It’s there in the schematic if you look. L1, 10u, sitting in the power section between the battery and the boost converter, wearing the wrong units.

I didn’t catch it in the part selector because the part selector is usually right, and I was clicking through it fast. The fix on the EVT was to short the inductor out — remove it and solder a wire across the pads. Signal integrity gets worse; on five boards, who cares.

The lesson isn’t “be careful.” The lesson is that the fab’s part matcher is a lossy, automated guess at what you meant, and it will confidently pick a completely different component class if your label is ambiguous. Write 10uH. Write the units. Then check the matcher’s output line by line, because it is not a design review.

I designed a screen I had no way to attach

The badge uses an LCD that terminates in a flat orange flex cable. I laid out a row of pads on the board and assumed JLC would solder the flex directly onto them.

Yolanda, my assembly contact, emailed asking how to orient it. I sent a photo. She checked with the factory and came back:

“We are sorry that we cannot solder this part as the pins are too dense.”

My options, per JLC: leave it unpopulated (and they won’t even ship the loose screens — customs trouble — so $8 storage or discard them), or find a different part.

Neither is a fix. The fix was mine: solder an FPC connector (LCSC C262585) onto those same pads, then plug the flex into the connector and tape the screen down. Pin-compatible. I offered to pay for the part and the labour.

Cost: $4.25.

That connector stayed in every revision after. It is the single cheapest thing on the board that saved the project.

The lesson: “the factory will figure it out” is not a manufacturing plan. Every part on your board needs a process that a person on a line can actually execute, and you should know what that process is before you order. I got lucky that the recovery was a $4 connector and not a respin.

The NFC receiver was on the wrong trace

Four days before the DVT order, doing a review I almost didn’t do, I found that the receive tap for the NFC antenna was connected to the second transmit line instead of the first.

Subtle. The board would have worked. Someone else had copied the same reference design, made the same mistake, and shipped it — with degraded read range they presumably never diagnosed.

I also added the ability to swap two pins on the NFC module between pull-up and pull-down, because the datasheet is in Chinese and I could not tell which the chip wanted. That’s not elegance, that’s buying an option on being wrong.

The mistakes that were the fab’s

Removing the colour silkscreen is not an acceptable answer

We were going to print full-colour artwork on the board. A whole illustrated landscape — canyon, waterfall, meadow — under the components.

It never got made. Here’s the chain of failures, all in about 48 hours:

One. JLC reviews the order and writes: standard PCBA orders get edge rails added by default, rails change the board dimensions, changed dimensions misalign your colour artwork, therefore we suggest removing the colour silkscreen option. Their rails add 5 mm per side. Their policy is they won’t print UV silkscreen that’s more than 2 mm off. So the default behaviour of their own process makes their premium feature impossible, and the resolution they proposed was that I drop the feature.

I asked instead whether they could realign it manually, and — more usefully — “How can this issue be avoided in the future? We need PCBA and we need the colour silkscreen option.”

Two. They come back with a different objection: the Gerbers weren’t generated by EasyEDA Pro, so the multi-colour silkscreen can’t be accepted at all. Cancel the order, refund, place a new one.

Three. I re-place it. It cancels again, this time because EasyEDA silently failed to save a component placement change I’d made.

So I invented a maneuver: place an order, let JLC generate their edge rails, cancel it, copy their rail geometry into my own file so the artwork is aligned to the final panel rather than the bare board, and re-order with “edge rails added by customer.” Same trick to launder KiCad output into EasyEDA.

It worked. It also cost days, three cancelled orders, and an overdrafted credit card at 3am.

And we still shipped without colour, because by then there was no schedule left to spend. The badge is purple soldermask and white silkscreen. It looks great. It is not what we designed, and part of the sponsorship conversation with EasyEDA had been built on a promise of multi-colour artwork.

The lesson: a fab feature that only works inside the vendor’s own EDA tool is not a feature, it’s a lock-in with a marketing page. Budget for it failing.

Flipped chips, untaped screens, and smoke

The PVT — 50 boards, purple, the first run that was supposed to look like the real thing — came back broken.

Three problems:

  1. The screens weren’t attached. Not badly attached. Not attached. On some units a piece of foam had been taped to the front of the screen. We had paid extra for the bonding step.
  2. Most of the boards wouldn’t turn on, because chips had been placed rotated.
  3. Some of them smoked if you put the batteries in backwards.

That last one is mine, not theirs. There was no reverse-polarity protection. Two AA cells in the wrong way and current goes somewhere it shouldn’t.

We hand-reworked all fifty. Alex and a friend did the physical work in Waterloo while I debugged over video from California, watching them chase boards that read 2.2 V on a rail that should have been 3. One board’s NFC chip got cooked when a rework went badly and the whole area had to be superheated. In the end every one of the fifty worked.

That is a genuinely bad way to spend a week. It is also exactly what the PVT is for. Fifty boards is a week of pain. Seventeen hundred is a cancelled event.

The complaint that actually worked

I wrote JLC a long email about the PVT. What made it work wasn’t the tone, it was the evidence: I attached five photos, named good_1, good_2, bad_1, bad_2, bad_3 — the correctly bonded screen from the previous order alongside what I’d just received, so there was no ambiguity about what “attached” meant.

And the most useful thing in it wasn’t a complaint at all. It was this question:

“What is the best way for us to document this requirement so it works well for your process — a dedicated assembly drawing, a process note on the order, attached reference photos, or referencing the previous order number? We’re happy to provide whatever makes it unambiguous on the production floor.”

Ask the factory how to tell the factory things. They know. Nobody does this, so they’re pleased when you do.

And then they told me something I didn’t know

Late in the production run, Vayne sent this unprompted:

“A few manually soldered battery holders had weak solder joints… The issue was caused by the metal tabs on the battery holder being difficult to solder, combined with a 1:1 pad-to-component land design. We recommend expanding the PCB pads by at least 0.5 mm on each side to allow sufficient solder wicking on the component pins.”

I had made the pads exactly the size of the part’s metal tabs. Solder needs somewhere to wick to. You want the pad bigger than the pin.

The part I find funny: I used JLC’s own footprint for that component. Their library gave me a footprint their factory then told me was hard to solder.

This is the single most valuable piece of engineering feedback I got all year, and it arrived by accident, in a support email, from a person who did not have to send it.

How you buy a part that doesn’t exist yet

The most software-engineer-brained assumption I brought to this project was that if a website shows a part in stock, the part is in stock.

Here is the same LED — WS2812B-2020, ~10,000 of them needed — quoted three times in three weeks:

DateUnit priceLead time
May 29$0.06506 business days
Jun 4$0.046929 business days
Jun 15$0.05798 business days

The cheapest quote was 28% cheaper and would have missed the event by weeks. In between those, two pre-orders were cancelled outright — one for a minimum-order-quantity problem, one because the supplier couldn’t actually deliver.

For a stretch in June the JLC stock page flipped between 22,000 units and 3,000 units on every other page refresh. I’m fairly sure they had none and the pre-order API was lying. I sat there at 3am trying to snipe a stock number that wasn’t real.

Lead time is a design constraint. It belongs in the schematic review next to voltage and current. A part you cannot get in eight days does not exist, no matter what the price column says.

Two other things JLC told me that I hadn’t thought to ask:

  • Screens and battery holders weren’t buyable through Global Sourcing at all — they’re local-supplier parts, which means the pre-order path, a 48-hour quote, and 6–18 days to warehouse.
  • The online build-time estimate doesn’t scale. It gave the same number for 5 units and 2,500. Machine setup, SMT, AOI, X-ray and QC all add real time that the quoting tool simply doesn’t model.

And: order attrition units. Not because parts fail, but because the line consumes them.

What the badge actually cost

$7.57 per badge in components, against a $7.50 target. Over by seven cents, on 1,715 populated boards. $12,985.80 of parts.

The shape of that number matters more than the number:

The screen is 34.9% of the badge. The ESP32 is 31.7%. Two parts are two-thirds of the cost. Everything else — every resistor, every capacitor, all seven buttons, the USB-C connector, the LDO — is rounding error.

I spent real hours early on finding a battery holder that saved $0.55 a unit. It turned out to be through-hole, with pins sticking out the back of a badge people were going to wear against their chest, so we reverted and paid $0.40/unit more than the plan. Meanwhile the screen alone was $2.27.

When two line items are 66% of your cost, that’s where the engineering goes. I knew that. I optimised the tail anyway, because the tail is where the easy wins feel like they are.

The PCBA order came to $14,338.40, plus $570.70 for the blank boards, against a $27,153 quote at 2,500 units and a $28,000 budget.

Testing 1,700 units you will never touch

The joints you can’t see

X-ray inspection comes with a PCBA order. I got the report for the production run: two components, nine sampled boards.

That’s the ESP32, photographed straight through its own metal can. Both dies are visible — the small rotated rectangle is flash, the big diamond is the SoC — along with the bond wires fanning out from them, the castellated pads around the module’s edge, and the PCB antenna meander running off to the left.

The point isn’t that it’s beautiful, though it is. It’s that the module sits on a large thermal pad underneath its body, and the other part they imaged is a leadless package with a pad in the middle. You cannot see either of those joints. No amount of squinting at a finished board tells you whether the solder under there wetted or whether it’s mostly a bubble. The pale circles inside the dark solder are voids — that’s the thing X-ray exists to find, and it’s the only way to find it short of destroying the board.

I did nothing to earn this. It’s just part of how they build. It is also, by a distance, the most sophisticated piece of inspection in the entire project — and I very nearly never opened the report, because I first went looking at these images because they were cool, not because I was checking anything.

The test I had to build myself

You cannot functionally test 1,700 badges yourself. So you have to make the factory do it, which means turning “does it work” into something a stranger can execute in fifteen seconds without speaking your language.

What I sent JLC: a firmware binary, flashed over USB-C, that boots straight into a guided self-test. Press every button — each one turns green on screen when it registers. LEDs flash red, green, blue. Then an internal self-test result.

Their quote: 9 hours for 2,000 boards at 15 seconds each — $67.32. Sixty-seven dollars to functionally test the entire run. That is absurdly cheap and I’d have paid ten times it.

Then Vayne pushed back on my instructions:

“Text-only instructions may be unclear for our operators. We would recommend providing a PDF with screenshots to make the process more intuitive.”

Correct, and slightly humbling. I had written a test procedure for an engineer. The people running it work a line and have never seen my board. I rewrote it as a screenshotted PDF.

I also set up first article inspection — build a small batch, test them, send results, wait for my approval before running the rest. It’s a checkbox; you just write it in the PCBA remark field. Worth knowing that “first article” doesn’t mean one unit: it’s a small batch, and large customers often run it on the first few thousand. I negotiated toward 25.

And then they skipped it.

I opened the order page one morning and the status said Packaging. No test results. No first-article report. The thing I’d paid for and specified in three separate emails had simply not happened.

I emailed and asked them to stop and confirm the testing status before shipping. They did eventually run it, and sent a video of operators on the line running my firmware on my boards, which is one of the more surreal things I’ve watched.

The lesson: a process you have specified is not a process that will happen. Build the checkpoint where you have to click something, or assume it was skipped.

Shipping is the schedule

The last six weeks were not engineering. They were logistics with an immovable date on the end of them.

Things that went wrong after the boards were built:

  • One board had a missing pad and couldn’t be repaired. JLC doesn’t remake assembled boards. They offered a $10 coupon; I told them to ship it anyway.
  • Cleaning the boards would strip the solder mask. They asked to skip it. I asked for a photo, because we were handing these to two thousand people. We settled on cleaning with a toothbrush wrapped in a cleanroom wipe, at $59.11 extra, with a warning that the solvent is corrosive and might discolour components near the joints.
  • 25 LCD screens came in defective. Of the replacements, 12 came from my own consigned stock and 13 from JLC’s.
  • One NFC chip failed testing and they pulled a replacement from my inventory to avoid stopping the line.

On August 12, with 1,676 boards prepared and 24 still blocked, I sent the email I’m proudest of in this whole project:

WE DO NOT NEED EVERY BOARD TO BE PERFECT

If a small number of boards (fewer than 100) are defective, that is completely acceptable to us. Around 1,600 working boards on time is far more valuable to us than 1,700 perfect boards arriving late.

If skipping the rework on the 25 defective parts would let the order ship this week, please skip the rework and ship. You have our approval to leave out, set aside, or ship as-is any boards that would slow down the shipment. Please treat speed as the top priority.

And I gave them the arithmetic instead of a deadline:

They must arrive by September 4. This date cannot move. Our last shipment took about one week from JLC. So the boards must leave JLC by August 20 at the very latest. We would like to aim for August 15.

Vendors cannot act on urgency. They can act on a date, a transit time, and an explicit statement of what you’re willing to give up. I also offered to reply at any hour, to pay any fee that bought speed, to move to WhatsApp, and to put a Mandarin speaker on a call.

They shipped on August 13. I tracked the actual 777 across the Pacific.

They landed on August 20 — 32-ish boxes, hand-numbered in marker, into a shipping container in Waterloo. Fifteen days before the event.

The batteries never went near that shipment. Two AA cells per badge, five thousand cells, bought in North America and shipped domestically. Partly because air-freighting batteries from China is a category of paperwork and risk I wanted nothing to do with, and partly for a much more practical reason: at an event, a dead badge needs to be fixable in ten seconds by handing someone two AAs. Not by charging it.

That’s also why it’s 2×AA and not a lithium cell. It’s a worse battery in almost every technical respect. It’s a far better battery for a thing two thousand strangers will carry for 36 hours.

The stuff that worked

Not everything was a fire.

Power. Early on the badge pulled 670 mA continuous, which at that discharge rate costs you roughly 70% of an alkaline cell’s usable capacity. I went through the hardware abstraction layer and made it aggressive about shutting peripherals down when nothing needed them. 182 mA. That’s about a 20% capacity loss instead of 70% — the batteries in the badge roughly tripled in usefulness without a single component change.

The safety silkscreen. After the smoke, the back of the board grew a set of instructions: use only AA alkaline batteries, carefully match and check the + / − markings before powering on. And remove batteries before boarding an airplane, keep the badge and batteries in your carry-on. Plus emergency numbers — campus police, a mental health line, first aid — because two thousand people are going to be carrying this thing for three days.

The de-smokifier. An ideal diode for reverse-battery protection, prototyped by hand-soldering it onto a PVT board with magnet wire and far too much flux, then designed into the production run. It cost $0.15 a unit. I profiled the power draw afterward to check it hadn’t hurt efficiency — it hadn’t, and the earlier measurement that suggested it had turned out to be a solder bridge on my test rig.

The firmware. ESP-IDF, LVGL for the UI, littlefs for storage, a Lua runtime so hackers can write apps, and a browser IDE to write them in. Each badge gets a human-readable identity — mine boots as brave-goose-plum-wind. It sleeps after five minutes and wakes on START.

Two classes of badge. 1,715 fully populated, 750 bare boards with no components. The blank ones cost about a tenth as much and get artwork where the screen would be. That decision alone is what made the numbers work.

What I’d do differently

Buy the bake. The LEDs are moisture-sensitive. The reflow profile can flash-boil moisture inside the package and crack them. JLC asked if I wanted board baking. I said no, to save time, on a run of five boards where it genuinely didn’t matter. The back-and-forth about not baking cost four days. It’s an $8 flat fee. On 15,000 LEDs it’s not optional anyway. Just buy it at order time and never discuss it.

Never let the vendor’s EDA tool own the artwork. The colour silkscreen died because it only existed inside EasyEDA, and EasyEDA lost my edits, mangled my imports, and rejected my Gerbers. If a feature requires me to hand my design to a tool I don’t trust, that feature is a nice-to-have and I should schedule it as one.

Check the fab’s own footprints. The battery holder pads came from JLC’s library and JLC’s factory told me they were wrong. Vendor footprints are a starting point, not an authority.

Order every part before designing around it. Almost every schedule scare this year was a sourcing scare, not an engineering one. The LED stock crisis, the screen pre-order, the battery holder that turned out to be through-hole. I’d pre-order the long-lead parts first and design to what actually arrived.

Write the provisioning tooling before the boards land. I built the flasher in the last week, under pressure, while also counting boxes. It works — it flashes badges and logs each MAC so we can tell how many we actually have. It should have existed in June, when I had nothing to do but wait for a boat.

Don’t skip the PVT. I’ll say it again because I nearly did. Fifty boards found flipped chips, missing screen adhesive, and a reverse-polarity failure mode. Discovering any one of those on the production run would have ended the project.

Where it stands

2,465 boards. 1,715 of them fully populated. Every one of them works except two.

Two dead boards out of 1,715 is a 0.12% failure rate. For comparison, the NorthSec badge team — eleven people, eleven months, thirteen consecutive years of doing this — run about 2.5%. I do not think I’m better at this than they are. I think I got a good factory, a first article inspection I had to fight for, and a lot of luck.

In five days, two thousand people pick these up at registration, put two AAs in the back, and turn them on.

I’ll let you know.
